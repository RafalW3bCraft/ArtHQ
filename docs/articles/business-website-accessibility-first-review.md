Before launching a business website, walk through the task you most want a visitor to complete. A focused first review can reveal problems you can describe clearly to your developer. It also helps your team see the website as a customer journey rather than a collection of finished-looking pages.

Imagine a potential customer finding your services, opening the contact page, and sending an enquiry. The example below is hypothetical. Use your own most important task if it is different, such as finding a shop address or requesting an appointment.

## Try the journey on a phone

Start with a phone you use regularly. Can you understand the service, find the next step, and complete the task? Record unexpected sideways scrolling, overlapping text, difficult controls, or instructions that disappear when the keyboard opens.

Write down what happened and which page you were using. A short description such as “the submit button is covered when I enter my message” gives the developer a clear starting point. Repeat on a larger screen; the two views can reveal different problems.

## Read every form label

Each field should explain what information belongs there. Required fields and formatting instructions should be understandable before submission. Placeholder text that disappears while someone types should not carry the only label.

Try a deliberate mistake, such as an incomplete email address. Check that the form explains the problem and gives you a way to fix it without unnecessarily losing the rest of your message. Google's web.dev [forms guidance](https://web.dev/learn/accessibility/forms) explains the role of visible labels, descriptions, and useful error feedback.

For your review notes, list the confusing field and the instruction you expected. Avoid collecting real customer information just to run this check.

## Follow the keyboard focus

On a computer, use the keyboard to move through links, buttons, and form fields. Watch for a visible indicator showing which element will respond. Does movement follow an understandable order? Can you reach the controls you need and leave any dialog you open?

The web.dev [keyboard focus guide](https://web.dev/learn/accessibility/focus) explains focus and navigation order. Your own review can record where the indicator disappears or the journey gets stuck. Ask a developer to investigate rather than guessing which technical change will fix it.

## Review images in context

An informative image needs a text alternative that conveys its purpose. A decorative image can be treated differently. Read each image together with the surrounding copy: what would a visitor miss if the image were unavailable?

For a diagram, that might mean describing the process it explains. For a team photo, the useful description depends on why the photograph is there. The web.dev [images module](https://web.dev/learn/accessibility/images) gives background on these choices. Keep a list of images whose purpose or description needs review.

## Check readability and structure

Look at body text, muted labels, links, and buttons against their backgrounds. Ask your developer to measure contrast; judging by appearance alone is limited. Increase browser zoom and repeat the enquiry journey, noting obscured content or controls. The [color and contrast guide](https://web.dev/learn/accessibility/color-contrast) is a useful reference for the readability discussion.

Read the headings as a separate outline. Do they describe the page and group its content sensibly? Ask the developer to check their underlying structure as well as their visual styling. The [content structure module](https://web.dev/learn/accessibility/structure) explains why semantic structure matters.

## Turn observations into a practical work list

Group your notes around the customer task. Include the page, the action attempted, what happened, and the expected outcome. Add screenshots where they help, taking care to exclude private information. Ask who will fix each issue and how you will confirm the repair.

Where possible, arrange feedback from people with disabilities and compensate participants appropriately. Their experiences can reveal barriers a general review misses.

This exercise is an initial review. It does not establish accessibility conformance or replace a thorough evaluation. Keep the task checklist and repeat it after significant changes, alongside the more complete checks your team agrees to perform.
