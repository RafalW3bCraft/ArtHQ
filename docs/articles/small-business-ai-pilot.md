A small business can begin evaluating AI with one clearly defined task and a person checking the results. That's the whole idea behind a pilot: pick something low-stakes, learn what the tool actually does in your hands, and only then decide whether it deserves a bigger role.

## Pick a task with manageable consequences

A useful first task is internal, repetitive, and easy to check. A good example: drafting a frequently-asked-questions page from notes your team has already approved. The source material exists, the answers are known, and mistakes are easy to spot because your staff already know the correct information.

Avoid anything that touches money, commitments, or customers directly. A first pilot should not send emails to clients, approve refunds, generate quotes, or post anything public. Those can come later, if the pilot earns it.

## Define the inputs before you start

Write down exactly what goes into the tool and what never does. For a FAQ draft, the inputs might be your approved product notes, your returns policy as written, and a style example. The forbidden list should include anything sensitive: customer names, payment details, employee records, passwords, contract terms, or unpublished financials.

This matters because of access boundaries. Whatever you paste into a tool leaves your control in some sense, and you should not assume any system handles your information privately or securely unless you have verified that yourself. The simplest safe rule for a pilot: if you wouldn't read it aloud to a stranger, don't type it into the tool.

## Name one accountable reviewer

Every draft needs a human owner — one named person who reads the output, checks it against the approved source material, and decides what happens next. This is not a formality. AI tools can produce fluent, confident text that is simply wrong, a behaviour often called hallucination. The tool might invent a policy you don't have, state a warranty period you never offered, or answer a question you deliberately leave open.

A draft is never approval. It is not permission to publish, not a payment instruction, not a business commitment. Treat every output the way you'd treat a new employee's first attempt: promising, maybe, but unverified.

## Test a small, representative set

Don't judge the tool on one good result. Pull together a small set of real examples — say, ten to twenty typical questions for the FAQ — and run the pilot against all of them. A representative set matters because tools often handle the easy cases well and fail on the odd ones. Include at least a couple of edge cases: a question with no answer in your notes, or one where the correct answer is "it depends."

## Record what actually happens

Keep simple notes. For each draft, record what the tool got wrong or missed, how long the reviewer spent fixing it, and how long the task takes by hand. That manual baseline is essential. Without it, you have no way to know whether the tool is saving effort or just moving the work from writing to correcting.

A mistake log also shows patterns. If the tool repeatedly invents details when your notes are silent on a topic, that's a predictable weakness you can plan around — or a reason to stop.

## Agree on stop conditions in advance

Before the pilot starts, decide what would make you abandon it. Reasonable stop conditions might include: drafts that invent facts more than occasionally, reviewer correction time that exceeds the time to write from scratch, or any output that reaches a customer with an error. Writing these down beforehand protects you from drifting into wider use just because the tool is already there.

## A hypothetical example

Imagine a small garden supply shop. The owner asks the tool to draft FAQ answers from the shop's approved notes on delivery areas, opening hours, and plant care. The reviewer finds eight of ten answers are usable with light edits, but the tool has invented a "30-day plant guarantee" that doesn't exist. The log shows the invention happens whenever the notes don't address a question directly. The owner can revise the instructions to ask for "not covered in our notes" when information is missing, then test again with fresh examples. A cleaner batch would be useful evidence, but would not prove future drafts are reliable. The human review stays in place.

## A brief note on frameworks

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology publishes an AI Risk Management Framework, which it describes as voluntary guidance for managing risks and considering trustworthiness in how AI is designed, used, and evaluated, along with a companion profile for generative AI. It's a useful reference if you want a more structured vocabulary for the steps above. Nothing here claims any tool meets that framework, and following this article is not compliance with anything — it's simply practical pilot advice.

## Deciding what comes next

When the pilot ends, you have three honest options: expand the task's scope, keep it as-is with its reviewer and rules, or drop it. All three are legitimate outcomes. Set a time and spending limit before starting. Even a pilot you decide to stop can then leave you with a concrete record of what worked, what failed, and what needs further investigation.
