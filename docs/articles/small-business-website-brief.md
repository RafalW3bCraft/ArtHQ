A website project becomes easier to discuss when you can explain what it should help a customer do. You can start with a short brief instead of a technical specification. Set aside an afternoon to gather what you know, mark the gaps, and give your designer a useful starting point. More research may follow; this first document gives that work direction.

The steps below are our suggested planning exercise. Adapt the scope to your business rather than treating the list as a fixed website package.

## Choose the first customer action

Write one sentence describing the main task you want to support: request a quote, book a conversation, find your opening hours, or buy a particular product. Secondary tasks can still exist. Naming a priority helps you decide what belongs near the top of the page.

Imagine a local plumbing business preparing its first website. In this hypothetical example, the owner wants visitors to call about service availability. That suggests a prominent phone number and a clear description of the area served. It does not require an elaborate booking system before the business is ready to operate one.

## List the questions customers actually ask

Describe your intended customer in a few sentences. Then write down questions you hear during enquiries. Which services are available? What information is needed for a quote? What happens after someone gets in touch?

Use approved answers as the foundation for page content. If an answer is uncertain, record who will confirm it. A visible content gap in a brief is easier to resolve than an invented claim already included in a design.

## Give each page a job

Start with a small page map. Your homepage can introduce the business and its main customer action. Service pages can explain what you offer and any relevant limitations. An about page can introduce the people behind the work. A contact page can show the next step.

This is a starting suggestion, not a requirement to build four separate pages. A focused one-page site may suit an early launch. More detailed services may need their own pages. Beside each proposed page, write the question it answers and the action it supports. Put optional ideas in a separate list so they can be considered without expanding the initial scope by accident.

## Gather material you can stand behind

Collect accurate service descriptions, opening hours, contact details, relevant photographs, and brand assets. Confirm permission to use images and testimonials before supplying them for publication. Record any restrictions alongside the files, and get specialist advice if the permission requirements are unclear.

Avoid placeholders that resemble real customer reviews or completed results. Your brief should distinguish approved material from examples and unfinished copy. Give each missing item an owner and a realistic follow-up date.

## Decide what an enquiry needs

Sketch the smallest form that gives your team enough information to respond. For an initial conversation, a name, contact method, and short message may be sufficient. Explain why any additional field is needed.

Also describe what happens after submission. Who receives the enquiry? Where can the customer see confirmation? What should they do if submission fails? A form is part of an operating process, so include the person responsible for checking it.

## Include a review checklist

Ask for the main customer journey to be checked on a phone and a larger screen. Include readable text, clear form labels, keyboard navigation, and understandable success and error messages. These are starting checks; your developer should help define a fuller accessibility and technical review appropriate to the site.

For content, Google Search Central recommends useful, reliable information created for people. Its [helpful content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) is a useful reference when reviewing whether a page answers a visitor's actual question. It does not promise a search ranking.

## Hand over one clear document

Put the goal, audience, page map, approved material, enquiry process, and review checklist into a short document. Name the person who can approve scope and the person who will maintain information after launch. Mark unresolved questions clearly.

The brief can change as you learn more. Its value is a shared record of the decisions you have made and the decisions still needed. That gives the next conversation something concrete to work from.
